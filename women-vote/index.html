Bangladesh 2026 · How men and women voted

How Women Voted: Evidence from Bangladesh’s 2026 Polling-Centre Results

In most countries there is no way to know how men and women voted differently, because ballots are secret and everyone votes at the same places. Bangladesh is unusual. Thousands of its polling centres serve only men or only women, and the Election Commission publishes results for each centre separately. That means the difference can be counted directly, from real votes, rather than estimated from a survey.

This page reports what those centres show. The short version: Jamaat-e-Islami did better among women than among men. But that is only true outside the big cities. Inside them the pattern runs the other way, and the national average hides that completely.

1 · How this is possible at all

Separate centres let us count men’s and women’s votes separately

Bangladesh runs a large number of sex-segregated polling centres. Some serve only male voters, some serve only female voters, and the rest are mixed. Because results are published one centre at a time, the votes cast at a men-only centre can be added up on their own, and the same for a women-only centre. No survey, no modelling, no guessing at who voted for whom. These are counted ballots.

The limit is that this only works where the segregation exists. Mixed centres cannot be split, so they tell us nothing about sex. Across the country there are 42,382 polling centres, and about 11,100 of them are single-sex. That is roughly a quarter.

The catch: single-sex centres are mostly an urban thing

Those single-sex centres are not scattered evenly across the country. They are heavily concentrated in cities. More than three-quarters of polling centres inside city corporations are single-sex. In villages, fewer than one in five are.

This matters more than it might first appear, so it is worth stating plainly. Villages hold about 80% of Bangladesh's voters but only a small share of its single-sex centres. Cities hold about 10% of voters but supply a large share of the centres we can actually read. Anything measured from single-sex centres therefore over-weights cities compared with the country as a whole. Later on this turns out to change the answer, not just the precision of it.

2 · What this page covers

226 seats, not the whole country

This analysis covers only the constituencies where single-sex centres hold at least 10% of the constituency's electorate. That is 226 of 297 seats. The other 71 are left out completely.

The reason for the cut is that coverage varies enormously from seat to seat. In some constituencies almost every centre is single-sex. In others there are two. Seven constituencies have none at all. If a seat has two single-sex centres out of a hundred and forty, then a "gender gap" calculated for that seat is not measuring the seat. It is measuring two buildings. Averaging those seats in alongside well-covered ones adds noise and nothing else.

Ten percent is a judgement call, not a law. So the obvious question is whether the finding depends on where the line is drawn. The table below moves the line and shows what happens.

Who the parties are

BNP — the Bangladesh Nationalist Party, the largest party in this election and the winner of most seats.

Jamaat — Jamaat-e-Islami, an Islamist party and the main challenger. It is the party this page is mostly about.

Islami Andolan — a second, smaller Islamist party. It matters here as a comparison: if women simply favoured religious parties in general, this one should have gained among women too.

NCP — the National Citizen Party, a newer party that polled strongly in some cities.

On Bangladeshi ballots parties are identified by a symbol as well as a name. Jamaat's is the scales (দাঁড়িপাল্লা), the BNP's is the sheaf of paddy (ধানের শীষ). The source data uses the symbols.

A note on “points”

Vote shares are given as a percentage of valid votes. Differences between two shares are given in percentage points, usually shortened to points.

If Jamaat took 31.2% of men's votes and 32.8% of women's, the difference is 1.6 points. It is not "1.6 percent", and it is not a 1.6% increase — as a proportion of Jamaat's own vote the change is about five percent. Points are used throughout because they are what matters for who wins.

3 · The main finding

The BNP got the same from both. Jamaat did not.

Take all 10,468 single-sex centres in the 226 seats. Add up every vote cast at a men's centre, then every vote cast at a women's centre, and compare the two.

The BNP comes out almost exactly level: 49.24% of the men's votes and 49.25% of the women's. That is a difference of one-hundredth of a point, which is to say no difference at all. Jamaat comes out 1.6 points higher among women, at 32.8% against 31.2%.

The BNP result is useful in its own right. It tells us the method is not manufacturing gaps out of nothing. If splitting votes this way produced a spurious difference for any large party, the BNP would show one. It does not.

Before going further it is worth being clear about what 1.6 points is and is not. It is not a landslide among women, and it does not mean women as a group backed Jamaat. Roughly two-thirds of women at these centres voted for someone else. What it means is that Jamaat's support was somewhat stronger among women than among men, by an amount that is large enough to matter in close seats and large enough to be well outside any plausible rounding or counting noise at this sample size.

4 · Ruling out the simplest explanation

It is not just that women prefer religious parties

The first explanation most people reach for is that women in Bangladesh lean towards religious parties in general, and Jamaat is simply picking up that tendency. There is a direct way to test this. Islami Andolan is also an Islamist party and it contested widely. If the explanation were right, it should show the same pattern.

It does not. Islami Andolan did worse among women than among men, by 0.4 points. So did the NCP, by 0.2 points. Jamaat is the only party in this data that women backed more than men did. Whatever is happening, it is specific to Jamaat rather than general to religious parties.

5 · Where the gap actually lives

Villages and small towns, yes. Big cities, the opposite.

The single national figure of 1.6 points hides a split large enough to change how the whole thing should be read. Every polling centre in Bangladesh sits in one of three kinds of administrative area: a union, which is rural; a municipality, which is a small town; or a city corporation, which is a big city. Splitting the same centres three ways gives three different answers.

  • Villages. Jamaat takes 31.4% of men's votes and 33.7% of women's. A gain of 2.4 points among women.
  • Small towns. 30.9% of men's votes and 33.3% of women's. A gain of 2.4 points, almost identical to villages.
  • Big cities. 31.0% of men's votes and 30.4% of women's. A loss of 0.5 points. Here it is the BNP that gains among women, by 1.2 points.

This is the most important thing on the page. Outside the big cities the effect is roughly half again as large as the national average suggests. Inside them it does not merely weaken — it reverses. Reporting one national number for a pattern that changes sign between places is misleading, however carefully the number itself is calculated.

It also explains why the national average sits at 1.6 rather than 2.4. Roughly a third of the electorate we can see is in city corporations, far more than their share of the country, and their negative pulls the average down.

6 · Could it just be location?

We checked men and women in the same building

There is a serious objection to everything so far. Men's centres and women's centres are different buildings in different spots. If women's centres happen to sit in the parts of a constituency that already favour Jamaat, then what looks like a difference between men and women could really be a difference between neighbourhoods.

The way to deal with this is to compare only within a single location. Some buildings host both a men's centre and a women's centre. Same address, same street, same catchment. Keeping only those and comparing inside them removes the objection, because location is then held fixed. That leaves 1,204 buildings containing 2,813 centres.

Why the comparison has to be Jamaat against the BNP

Inside the same building, women give both big parties slightly more than men do. This is not mysterious. Women's ballots go less often to small parties and independents, so the two leaders each pick up a little of what the small parties lose. Looking at Jamaat's share on its own would credit Jamaat with something that is really just this concentration effect.

The honest measure is therefore Jamaat's lead over the BNP, and how far that lead moves between the men's side and the women's side of the same building. If Jamaat gains only because of concentration, its lead over the BNP will not move.

Split by kind of place, that measure repeats the earlier pattern closely. Jamaat's lead grows among women in villages and in small towns, and shrinks in big cities. All three results are statistically significant, meaning they are far too large to be chance.

Why this chart is never combined into one bar

Combining the three rows into a single figure gives −0.5 points, the opposite sign to two of the three groups it is made from. That is not a paradox in the data. It is a well-known statistical trap called Simpson's paradox, and it happens whenever the groups being combined are mixed in very different proportions from the population.

Here the reason is straightforward. Buildings large enough to hold both a men's and a women's centre are usually big-city buildings. So the matched sample is 41% big-city, against 31% of the centres in scope and around 10% of the country's voters. The one group where the effect is negative dominates the combined figure and flips it.

This is worth spelling out because an earlier version of this analysis reported the combined figure and drew the wrong conclusion from it. The three rows are the result. The combined number is an artefact of who ends up in the sample.

One thing this test does not do, despite how such tests are usually described: it does not remove geography from the picture. It holds geography fixed within each kind of place, which is a narrower and more honest claim. The gap is real inside villages and inside towns, and it is genuinely negative inside cities.

7 · The strongest region

The gap is widest in the south-west

Within the 226 seats, the gap is far from evenly spread. In a typical seat men and women voted almost identically: the median seat gap is +0.2 points, which is effectively nothing. The national figure is driven by a minority of seats where the gap is large.

Those seats cluster in one place. Across the Khulna division in the south-west — the Jessore, Satkhira and Jhenaidah belt — women take Jamaat from 46% to past 50%. Crossing 50% matters, because in a two-way contest it is the difference between coming second and winning. In the rest of the country the same comparison moves Jamaat only about a point, from roughly 29.5% to 30.5%.

8 · The same region won Jamaat its seats

The south-west gave Jamaat much of what it won

The south-west is a small part of the country. It is 13% of the seats covered here. But Jamaat won 18 of the 29 covered seats in it, against a much thinner rate everywhere else, and those 18 make up 38% of everything it won inside this scope.

So the region where women moved most towards Jamaat is also the region that delivered Jamaat its seats. That is suggestive, but on its own it is not evidence of anything causal. It could simply be that Jamaat is strong in the south-west, full stop, and that both facts are separate consequences of that strength. The next section tests exactly that.

9 · The key test

The pattern holds outside that region too

The worry from the last section is that "Jamaat does better with women" and "Jamaat wins seats" might both just be "Jamaat is strong in Khulna", counted twice. To check, the seats are split two ways at once: by region, and by whether Jamaat actually won them.

If the region were the whole story, then outside the south-west there should be no relationship between the women's gap and whether Jamaat won. There is one. Even outside the belt, the gap is about five times larger in seats Jamaat won than in seats it lost — +2.5 points against +0.5.

Viewed a different way: across all covered seats the average gap is about 1.3 points, but in the seats Jamaat actually won it is 3.9, and in the seats the BNP won it is 0.7. Where Jamaat was competitive, women moved towards it considerably more than the headline figure suggests.

A caution on direction. This shows the gap and winning travel together. It does not establish that the gap caused the wins. Jamaat winning a seat and women favouring Jamaat in that seat may both follow from the party being organisationally strong there. Nothing in this data can separate those.

10 · Turnout

Fewer women voted, and they still chose Jamaat more

One more alternative explanation is worth closing off. If turnout among women had been unusually high, the difference might reflect who turned out rather than what they preferred — an unusual surge of Jamaat-leaning women, say.

The opposite happened. Turnout at women's centres was 51.6% against 57.0% at men's, a gap of about five and a half points. Fewer women voted, and the ones who did still gave Jamaat a larger share. That points to preference rather than mobilisation.

11 · What if only one side voted?

On women’s votes alone, Jamaat leads more of these seats

A natural way to feel the size of the effect is to ask what would happen if only one sex had voted. Take each of the 226 covered seats. Count it using only its men's centres, and see who leads. Then count it again using only its women's centres.

On men's votes Jamaat leads 46 of the seats. On women's votes it leads 57. In 15 seats the men's centres lean BNP while the women's centres lean Jamaat, and 4 go the other way.

How to read those numbers, and how not to

The temptation is to treat 46 and 57 as two possible parliaments. They are not, for three separate reasons, and the chart includes the official result specifically so the difference is visible.

  • These are only 226 of 297 seats, so no row is a parliament in the first place.
  • Each row counts only single-sex centres, which is around 30% of the electorate in those seats. The remaining 70% voted at mixed centres and is not in any of these numbers.
  • Those centres lean towards Jamaat compared with the full count. Counting the same single-sex centres together, with both sexes included, puts Jamaat ahead in 54 seats. The official result, counting every centre, gave it 47. So the basis is already about seven seats friendlier to Jamaat before anything is split by sex.

The meaningful quantity is the distance between the bottom two rows — 46 against 57 — because both are measured on exactly the same centres, so the urban bias affects them equally and cancels out. The distance is real. The levels are not forecasts of anything.

In short

What to remember

  • Jamaat is the only party here that women backed more than men did — by about 1.6 points across the 226 covered seats. The BNP was level to within one-hundredth of a point.
  • It is not a general preference for religious parties. Islami Andolan, the other Islamist party, did worse among women, not better.
  • The gap is a village and small-town pattern. About 2.4 points in each. In the big cities it runs the other way, with Jamaat losing ground among women and the BNP gaining.
  • That split is not an artefact of where the sample line is drawn. Move the coverage cut anywhere from 0% to 30% and the three place-level answers barely change, even though the combined figure slides around.
  • Holding location fixed gives the same split. Comparing men's and women's centres in the same building, Jamaat's lead over the BNP grows among women outside the cities and shrinks inside them.
  • It is concentrated, not national. The median seat shows almost no gap. The widest gaps are in the south-west, where women push Jamaat past 50%.
  • That region also produced much of what Jamaat won — 18 of 29 covered seats there, 38% of its wins in scope from 13% of the seats. The link survives outside the region too.
  • Fewer women voted, not more. Turnout at women's centres was 5.4 points lower, so this is about preference rather than turnout.
  • This is not a national result, and none of it is a projection. It covers 226 of 297 seats, and within those only the roughly 30% of voters who use single-sex centres — a more urban group than the country as a whole. Those centres lean more towards Jamaat than the full official count does, so the seat numbers here describe the voters we can see and nothing more.

Scope

Every figure on this page covers the 226 constituencies where single-sex polling centres hold at least 10% of the constituency's electorate. Coverage inside those seats runs from 10% to 98%, with a median of 22%. The remaining 71 seats are excluded, including seven that contain no single-sex centre at all. Figures are computed from 10,468 single-sex centres — 5,247 men's and 5,221 women's — holding about 30% of the electorate of the covered seats. Mixed centres cannot be split by sex and are never used.

Known bias in the sample

Sex-segregated polling is largely an urban practice. 77% of city-corporation centres are single-sex, against 42% in municipalities and 18% in unions, so this sample substantially over-represents cities relative to the country. Measured across all 297 seats, counting only single-sex centres understates the BNP by 1.7 points, overstates the NCP by 2.2 points, and picks a different winner from the full official result in 23 of the 290 seats that have any coverage. For that reason seat counts on this page are presented against the same centres counted together, never against the official result, and never as a projection.

Choice of the 10% cut

The threshold is a judgement, and the table in section 2 shows its effect. Between a 0% and a 30% cut the combined figure moves from +1.7 to +0.8 points, but the village, small-town and big-city figures stay close to +2.3, +2.35 and −0.6 throughout. The movement in the combined figure tracks the rising share of big-city centres in the sample, from 29% to 51%, rather than any change in the underlying pattern.

Same-building test

Buildings are matched on the exact centre name within a constituency, normalised to Bengali letters and digits only. Parenthetical qualifiers such as (বালক শাখা) and (বালিকা শাখা) are kept rather than stripped, because those denote separate premises. Stripping them roughly doubles the matched sample but merges different sites, and reverses the result. 1,204 buildings containing 2,813 centres qualify. Confidence intervals are 95% on the mean of the per-building differences.

The test is reported by kind of place and is never pooled. Pooled across all three it returns −0.5 points, the opposite sign to two of its three components, because the matched sample is 41% big-city buildings against 31% of the centres in scope. This is Simpson's paradox rather than a finding.

Other definitions

Vote shares are of valid votes. Party columns available in the source are eight ballot symbols, together about 91% of the national vote. District charts use districts with at least 6 single-sex centres of each sex. "South-west" means the Khulna division: Bagerhat, Chuadanga, Jashore, Jhenaidah, Khulna, Kushtia, Magura, Meherpur, Narail and Satkhira. Seat winners are the full official count across all parties. Village, small town and big city are the official union, municipality and city-corporation classification of each polling centre.
