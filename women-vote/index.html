Bangladesh 2026 · How men and women voted

How Women Voted: Evidence from Bangladesh’s 2026 Polling-Centre Results

In most countries there is no way to know how men and women voted differently. Ballots are secret and everyone votes at the same places, so the question is normally answered by survey. Bangladesh is unusual: thousands of its polling centres serve only men or only women, and results are published for each centre separately. The difference can therefore be counted directly, from real ballots.

This page reports what those centres show. In short, Jamaat-e-Islami did better among women than among men — clearly so in villages and small towns, and not at all in the big cities, where the movement runs the other way. A single national figure hides that completely.

1 · How this is possible at all

Separate centres let us count men’s and women’s votes separately

Bangladesh runs a large number of sex-segregated polling centres. Some serve only male voters, some only female voters, and the rest are mixed. Because results are published one centre at a time, the votes cast at a men-only centre can be totalled on their own, and the same for a women-only centre. No survey, no modelling, no inference about who voted for whom. These are counted ballots.

The limit is that this only works where the segregation exists. Mixed centres cannot be split and tell us nothing about sex. Of the country's 42,382 polling centres, about 11,100 are single-sex — roughly a quarter.

The catch: single-sex centres are mostly an urban practice

Those centres are not scattered evenly. They are concentrated in cities. More than three-quarters of polling centres inside city corporations are single-sex; in villages fewer than one in five are.

This matters more than it first appears. Villages hold about 80% of Bangladesh's voters but supply a small share of the readable centres. Cities hold about 10% of voters and supply a large share. Anything measured this way therefore over-weights cities. Later this turns out to change the answer, not merely its precision.

2 · What this page covers

226 seats, not the whole country

This analysis covers only the constituencies where single-sex centres hold at least 10% of the constituency's electorate — 226 of 297 seats. The other 71 are left out completely.

Coverage varies enormously between seats. In some almost every centre is single-sex; in others there are two, and seven constituencies have none at all. If a seat has two single-sex centres out of a hundred and forty, a gender gap calculated for it is not measuring the seat, it is measuring two buildings. Averaging those in alongside well-covered seats adds noise and nothing else.

Who the parties are

BNP — the Bangladesh Nationalist Party, the largest party in this election and the winner of most seats. It led its own coalition.

Jamaat — Jamaat-e-Islami, an Islamist party and the main challenger, leading the second coalition. It is the party this page is mostly about.

NCP — the National Citizen Party, a newer party that polled strongly in some cities. It stood inside the Jamaat-led coalition, which matters in section 6.

Islami Andolan — a second, smaller Islamist party, leading a separate coalition of its own. It serves as the comparison case: if women simply favoured religious parties, this one should have gained among women too.

Bangladeshi ballots identify parties by symbol as well as name — Jamaat's is the scales (দাঁড়িপাল্লা), the BNP's the sheaf of paddy (ধানের শীষ) — and the source data uses symbols. One symbol in the charts below, the rickshaw, is recorded in the official file only under the abbreviation KM, so it is labelled by its symbol here rather than by a name we cannot verify.

A note on “points”, and on the ranges

Vote shares are a percentage of valid votes. Differences between two shares are given in percentage points, shortened to points. If Jamaat took 31.2% of men's votes and 32.8% of women's, the difference is 1.6 points — not "1.6 percent", and not a 1.6% increase, since as a proportion of Jamaat's own vote it is about five percent.

Most figures here carry a 95% range, written like +1.6 (1.1 to 2.2). It means the true value is very likely somewhere in that span. Where a range crosses zero, the result is consistent with no difference at all and is described that way rather than as a finding. The ranges come from resampling whole constituencies rather than individual centres, because centres in the same seat are not independent of one another — this gives wider, more honest ranges.

3 · The main finding

The BNP got the same from both. Jamaat did not.

Take all 10,468 single-sex centres in the 226 seats. Total every vote cast at a men's centre, then every vote cast at a women's centre, and compare.

The BNP comes out level: 49.24% of men's votes and 49.25% of women's, a difference of one-hundredth of a point, with a range of −0.5 to +0.5 that sits squarely across zero. Jamaat comes out 1.6 points higher among women, at 32.8% against 31.2%, with a range of 1.1 to 2.2 that does not come near zero.

The BNP result is useful in itself. It shows the method is not manufacturing differences out of nothing. If splitting votes this way produced a spurious gap for any large party, the BNP would show one. It does not.

It is worth being clear about what 1.6 points is and is not. It is not a landslide among women, and it does not mean women as a group backed Jamaat — roughly two-thirds of women at these centres voted for someone else. It means Jamaat's support was somewhat stronger among women than among men, by an amount large enough to matter in close seats and well outside what counting noise could produce.

4 · Ruling out the simplest explanation

It is not that women prefer religious parties

The explanation most people reach for first is that women in Bangladesh lean towards religious parties generally, and Jamaat simply picks that up. There is a direct test. Islami Andolan is also an Islamist party and contested widely, so it should show the same pattern.

It does not. Islami Andolan did worse among women, by 0.4 points, as did the NCP by 0.2. Jamaat is the only party in this data that women backed more than men did. Whatever is happening is specific to Jamaat rather than general to religious parties.

This holds under a harder test. The next section splits the country into villages, small towns and big cities, and Islami Andolan loses ground among women in all three by almost the same amount each time. A national average can hide a great deal; three separate averages pointing the same way hide much less.

5 · Where the gap actually lives

Villages and small towns, yes. Big cities, not at all.

The national figure of 1.6 points hides a split large enough to change how the whole thing should be read. Every polling centre sits in one of three kinds of administrative area: a union, which is rural; a municipality, a small town; or a city corporation, a big city. Splitting the same centres three ways gives three different answers.

  • Villages. Jamaat takes 31.4% of men's votes and 33.7% of women's — a gain of 2.4 points, range 1.8 to 2.9. Comfortably clear of zero.
  • Small towns. 30.9% against 33.3% — a gain of 2.4 points, range 1.6 to 3.2. Almost identical to villages.
  • Big cities. 31.0% against 30.4% — a fall of 0.5 points, but with a range of −1.3 to +0.2 that crosses zero. On Jamaat's own share alone this is not a significant result: only 35 of the covered seats contain city-corporation centres, so the estimate is imprecise.

That last point deserves care, because it is easy to overstate. Jamaat's own share in the cities is statistically indistinguishable from flat. What is clearly significant in the cities is that the BNP gains — up 1.2 points among women, range 0.4 to 2.0 — and that the gap between the two parties therefore moves towards the BNP. The honest summary is not "Jamaat loses ground among city women" but "the advantage Jamaat enjoys elsewhere is absent in the cities, and if anything the movement there favours the BNP."

Either way the practical conclusion is the same, and it is the most important thing on the page: outside the big cities the effect is half again as large as the national average suggests, and inside them it is not there. Reporting one national number for a pattern that behaves this differently between places is misleading however carefully the number itself is calculated.

What every party did, in each kind of place

Looking at Jamaat alone leaves a question open: when women in the big cities did not go to Jamaat, where did they go? And is the city pattern something general about women in cities, or specific to these two parties? The chart below runs the same three-way split for every party of any size.

The city difference is a straight swap between the two leading parties. The NCP is flat at +0.1 and the smaller parties drift slightly down; the movement is between Jamaat and the BNP. Outside the cities the same swap runs the other way, Jamaat up about 2.4 points with the BNP down 0.4 in villages and 0.8 in small towns. This is not women in cities behaving differently in general.

The second thing to note is Islami Andolan, which loses ground among women in all three kinds of place and by almost the same amount each time: 0.4 points in villages, 0.3 in small towns, 0.4 in big cities. That is what makes the argument in the previous section hard to escape.

6 · A harder test of the same thing

Most of the shift crosses coalition lines

There is a complication worth taking seriously. Jamaat did not stand alone: it led a coalition, and the NCP and two smaller symbols stood inside it. If women moved to Jamaat mostly from Jamaat's own allies, that would be reshuffling within a bloc rather than a shift towards it, and it would mean much less.

Testing it is straightforward. Add up every party in the Jamaat-led coalition and treat them as one, then do the same for the BNP-led coalition, and repeat the comparison.

The finding survives. Nationally, Jamaat alone gains 1.6 points among women while its whole coalition gains 1.3 — so about a fifth of Jamaat's gain came from inside its own bloc, and four fifths from outside it. In villages and small towns the internal share is smaller still, 11% and 5%. The bloc-level gain remains clearly significant in both.

In the big cities the bloc view is actually sharper than the party view. Jamaat's own share there could not be distinguished from flat, but its whole coalition is down 0.8 points among women with a range of −1.5 to −0.02, and the BNP-led coalition is up 1.3 with a range of 0.4 to 2.1. Both clear zero. The city reversal is a firmer result at the level of blocs than at the level of single parties.

7 · Could it just be location?

We checked men and women in the same building

There is a serious objection to everything so far. Men's and women's centres are different buildings in different spots. If women's centres happen to sit in the parts of a constituency that already favour Jamaat, what looks like a difference between men and women could really be a difference between neighbourhoods.

The way to deal with this is to compare only within one location. Some buildings host both a men's and a women's centre — same address, same street, same catchment. Keeping only those removes the objection, because location is then held fixed. That leaves 1,204 buildings containing 2,813 centres.

Why the comparison has to be Jamaat against the BNP

Inside the same building, women give both big parties slightly more than men do. This is not mysterious: women's ballots go less often to small parties and independents, so the two leaders each pick up a little of what the small parties lose. In villages the five main parties take about 1.3 points more of women's ballots than of men's, and in small towns 1.2 — though in big cities only 0.03. Measuring Jamaat's share on its own would credit it with part of that concentration.

The honest measure is therefore Jamaat's lead over the BNP, and how far that lead moves between the men's and women's sides of the same building. If Jamaat gains only through concentration, its lead over the BNP will not move at all.

Split by kind of place, it repeats the earlier pattern closely, and here all three results are significant — including the cities, where this test is more precise than the share comparison was.

Why this chart is never combined into one bar

Combining the three rows gives −0.5 points, the opposite sign to two of the three groups it is made from. That is not a paradox in the data but a well-known trap called Simpson's paradox, which appears whenever combined groups are mixed in very different proportions from the population.

The reason here is simple. Buildings large enough to hold both a men's and a women's centre are usually big-city buildings, so the matched sample is 41% big-city against 31% of the centres in scope and around 10% of the country's voters. The one group where the effect is negative dominates the combined figure and flips it.

This is worth spelling out because an earlier version of this analysis reported the combined figure and drew the wrong conclusion from it. The three rows are the result; the combined number is an artefact of who ends up in the sample.

One thing this test does not do, despite how such tests are usually described: it does not remove geography from the picture. It holds geography fixed within each kind of place, which is a narrower and more honest claim.

8 · How the gap is spread

A minority of seats carries most of it

An average of 1.6 points could describe two very different worlds: every seat shifting a little, or a few seats shifting a great deal. It is the second.

The median seat has a gap of just +0.2 points, effectively nothing, while the mean is 1.34. That difference between median and mean is the signature of a long tail. The twenty highest seats alone account for about 45% of the total positive gap, and 117 of the 226 seats sit above zero at all — barely more than half.

This is why the phrase "women in Bangladesh favoured Jamaat" would be wrong even within these 226 seats. In most of them men and women voted close to identically. The national figure is produced by a concentrated minority of constituencies, and the next section shows where they are.

9 · Where those seats are

The gap is widest in the south-west

The seats carrying the tail cluster in one region. Across the Khulna division in the south-west — the Jessore, Satkhira and Jhenaidah belt — women take Jamaat from 46% to past 50%. Crossing 50% matters, because in a two-way contest it is the difference between second place and winning. In the rest of the country the same comparison moves Jamaat about a point, from roughly 29.5% to 30.5%.

District by district shows the same concentration. The top of the list is dominated by the south-west. The bottom, where women gave Jamaat less than men did, is mostly urban and eastern districts.

10 · The gap and Jamaat’s strength

The stronger Jamaat was, the larger the gap

The south-west is also where Jamaat won most: 18 of the 29 covered seats there, which is 38% of everything it won in scope, from 13% of the seats. So the region where women moved most is also the region that delivered its seats.

That could be one fact rather than two. Sorting seats by whether Jamaat won them risks exactly this: picking groups using the outcome, which can manufacture a pattern. A cleaner approach is to ignore winning entirely and use Jamaat's actual vote share in the seat, which is a continuous measure and does not involve choosing a threshold.

Done that way the relationship is smooth and rises the whole way. Where Jamaat polled under 10% the gap is zero; where it polled over 50% the gap is 4.5 points. The correlation across all 226 seats is 0.40. Because there is no threshold anywhere in this, the pattern cannot be an artefact of conditioning on who won.

The same thing shows up in the region split. Even outside the south-west, the gap is about five times larger in seats Jamaat won than in seats it lost — +2.5 points against +0.5 — so this is not simply the Khulna belt counted twice.

A caution on direction, which the data cannot settle. This shows that the gap and Jamaat's strength travel together. It does not show that the gap produced the strength. A party that is well organised in a place may both poll well there and appeal particularly to women there, with neither causing the other. Nothing here separates those.

11 · Turnout

Fewer women voted, and they still chose Jamaat more

One more alternative explanation is worth closing off. If turnout among women had been unusually high, the difference might reflect who turned out rather than what they preferred.

The opposite happened. Turnout at women's centres was 51.6% against 57.0% at men's, a gap of about five and a half points. Fewer women voted, and those who did still gave Jamaat a larger share, which points to preference rather than mobilisation.

The turnout gap is itself uneven, and in a way worth noting. It widens steadily from village to city: 4.1 points in villages, 5.9 in small towns and 7.3 in big cities. Overall turnout is also far lower in cities — about 48% against 61% in villages. So the places where we can read the most centres are also the places where the fewest people voted, which is another reason the urban part of this sample deserves caution.

12 · What if only one side voted?

On women’s votes alone, Jamaat leads more of these seats

A natural way to feel the size of the effect is to ask what would happen if only one sex had voted. Take each of the 226 covered seats, count it using only its men's centres and see who leads, then count it again using only its women's.

On men's votes Jamaat leads 44 seats. On women's it leads 55. In 15 seats the men's centres lean BNP while the women's lean Jamaat, and 4 go the other way.

Every row of the chart below, including the three single-sex rows, lets all eight parties the source records compete for the lead. A third party leads the single-sex centres in 11 of the 226 seats, mostly the NCP, so those rows are not a forced choice between two parties. That matters for comparing them with the official row.

Why the official row still has more “someone else” than the others

Other parties won 23 of these 226 seats officially, but only 11 to 13 lead at the single-sex centres. The difference is not that other parties were left out of the arithmetic — vote shares here are of all valid votes, so every ballot is in the denominator. There are two real reasons.

  • Sixteen of those 23 winners are invisible to this data. The single-sex source records only eight ballot symbols, about 91% of the national vote. Independents and smaller parties outside those eight have no column at all, so they cannot lead any row no matter how the lead is defined. Seven of the 23 — five NCP, one truck symbol, one hat symbol — are recorded and do appear.
  • The single-sex centres are a different electorate from the seat. They are about 30% of the voters in these constituencies and lean urban, so a party that won a seat on the full count need not lead the subset, and vice versa. The all-party rule matches the official winner in 192 of the 226 seats, or 85%.

How to read those numbers, and how not to

The temptation is to treat 44 and 55 as two possible parliaments. They are not, for three separate reasons, and the chart includes the official result so the difference is visible.

  • These are only 226 of 297 seats, so no row is a parliament in the first place.
  • Each row counts only single-sex centres, about 30% of the electorate in those seats. The other 70% voted at mixed centres and appears in none of these numbers.
  • Those centres lean towards Jamaat compared with the full count. Counting the same single-sex centres together, both sexes included, puts Jamaat ahead in 52 seats, while the official result, counting every centre, gave it 47. The basis is already several seats friendlier to Jamaat before anything is split by sex.

The meaningful quantity is the distance between the bottom two rows, 44 against 55, because both are measured on exactly the same centres by the same rule, so the urban bias affects them equally and cancels. The distance is real. The levels forecast nothing.

That distance is also unaffected by the choice of rule. Comparing only Jamaat against the BNP and ignoring third parties — which is what an earlier version of this chart did — gives 46 and 57 instead of 44 and 55. The gain is +11 seats either way, and the flip counts are 15 and 4 either way. The all-party rule is used here because it makes the rows comparable with the official result, not because it changes the answer.

The 15 seats where the sexes disagree are below. In each, the BNP is ahead at the men's centres and behind at the women's. The label on the right is who actually won on the full official count, and it is worth looking at: in several the official margin was not close. These are seats where men and women at single-sex centres disagreed, not seats that changed hands.

In short

What to remember

  • Jamaat is the only party here that women backed more than men did — by 1.6 points across the 226 covered seats, range 1.1 to 2.2. The BNP was level to within one-hundredth of a point.
  • It is not a general preference for religious parties. Islami Andolan, the other Islamist party, did worse among women — in villages, in small towns and in cities alike.
  • The gap is a village and small-town pattern. About 2.4 points in each, clearly significant. In the big cities it is absent: Jamaat's own share there cannot be told apart from flat, and what is significant is that the BNP gains instead.
  • Most of the shift crosses coalition lines. Jamaat's allies contributed about a fifth of its gain nationally, and only a tenth in villages, so this is not reshuffling inside a bloc.
  • The split is not an artefact of the sample cut. Move the coverage threshold anywhere from 0% to 30% and the three place-level answers barely change, even as the combined figure slides around.
  • Holding location fixed gives the same split. Comparing men's and women's centres in the same building, Jamaat's lead over the BNP grows among women outside the cities and shrinks inside them.
  • A minority of seats carries it. The median seat shows almost no gap; the top twenty seats account for about 45% of the total. The widest are in the south-west, where women push Jamaat past 50%.
  • The gap tracks Jamaat's strength smoothly — near zero where it polled under 10%, 4.5 points where it polled above 50%. Whether that is cause or consequence, this data cannot say.
  • Fewer women voted, not more — turnout was 5.4 points lower, and the shortfall widens from village to city. So this is about preference rather than turnout.
  • On women's votes alone Jamaat leads 55 of these seats against 44 on men's, a gain of 11. But the same centres counted together give it 52 while the official count gave it 47, so read the 11-seat distance, not the levels.
  • This is not a national result, and none of it is a projection. It covers 226 of 297 seats, and within those only the roughly 30% of voters who use single-sex centres — a more urban group than the country. Those centres lean more towards Jamaat than the full official count does, so the seat numbers describe the voters we can see and nothing more.

Scope

Every figure covers the 226 constituencies where single-sex polling centres hold at least 10% of the constituency's electorate. Coverage inside those seats runs from 10% to 98%, median 22%. The remaining 71 seats are excluded, including seven containing no single-sex centre at all. Figures come from 10,468 single-sex centres — 5,247 men's and 5,221 women's — holding about 30% of the electorate of the covered seats. Mixed centres cannot be split by sex and are never used.

Known bias in the sample

Sex-segregated polling is largely an urban practice: 77% of city-corporation centres are single-sex against 42% in municipalities and 18% in unions, so this sample substantially over-represents cities. Measured across all 297 seats, counting only single-sex centres understates the BNP by 1.7 points, overstates the NCP by 2.2, and picks a different winner from the full official result in 23 of the 290 seats with any coverage. Seat counts are therefore presented against the same centres counted together, never against the official result, and never as a projection.

Uncertainty

Ranges are 95% intervals from a bootstrap that resamples whole constituencies with replacement, 3,000 times, rather than individual centres. Centres within a seat share candidates, campaigns and local conditions, so treating them as independent would understate uncertainty considerably. Where an interval crosses zero the result is described as not distinguishable from no difference, rather than reported as a finding. The big-cities figures rest on only 35 seats and are correspondingly imprecise.

Choice of the 10% cut

The threshold is a judgement, and the table in section 2 shows its effect. Between a 0% and a 30% cut the combined figure moves from +1.7 to +0.8 points, while the village, small-town and big-city figures stay near +2.3, +2.35 and −0.6 throughout. The movement tracks the rising share of big-city centres in the sample, from 29% to 51%, rather than any change in the underlying pattern.

Coalitions

Coalition membership comes from the official results file. The Jamaat-led coalition here comprises Jamaat, the NCP and the rickshaw and wall-clock symbols; the BNP-led coalition comprises the BNP and the hat symbol. Islami Andolan led a separate coalition and is counted with neither. Bloc figures are the summed votes of member symbols as a share of valid votes.

Same-building test

Buildings are matched on the exact centre name within a constituency, normalised to Bengali letters and digits only. Parenthetical qualifiers such as (বালক শাখা) and (বালিকা শাখা) are kept rather than stripped, because they denote separate premises; stripping them roughly doubles the matched sample but merges different sites and reverses the result. 1,204 buildings containing 2,813 centres qualify. Intervals there are 95% on the mean of per-building differences.

The test is reported by kind of place and never pooled. Pooled across all three it returns −0.5 points, the opposite sign to two of its three components, because the matched sample is 41% big-city buildings against 31% of the centres in scope. That is Simpson's paradox rather than a finding.

Seat leads

A seat's "lead" at single-sex centres is whichever of the eight recorded symbols takes the most votes across those centres, so a third party can lead a row exactly as it can win a seat officially. An earlier version compared only Jamaat against the BNP and forced every seat to one of the two; that understated third parties by 11 to 13 seats per row but changed neither the men-to-women gain (+11 seats either way) nor the flip counts (15 and 4 either way).

The official row nonetheless shows more non-BNP, non-Jamaat winners than the single-sex rows do, for two reasons. Sixteen of those 23 winners took a ballot symbol the single-sex source does not record at all, so they are undetectable here under any rule; the other seven are recorded and do appear. And the single-sex centres are a different, more urban electorate than the whole seat, so the two need not agree — the all-party rule matches the official winner in 192 of the 226 seats, or 85%.

Other definitions

Vote shares are of valid votes, meaning all valid ballots including those cast for symbols the source does not break out. Party columns available in the source are eight ballot symbols, together about 91% of the national vote. District charts use districts with at least 6 single-sex centres of each sex. "South-west" means the Khulna division: Bagerhat, Chuadanga, Jashore, Jhenaidah, Khulna, Kushtia, Magura, Meherpur, Narail and Satkhira. Seat winners are the full official count across all parties. Village, small town and big city are the official union, municipality and city-corporation classification of each centre. The rickshaw symbol appears in the source only under the abbreviation KM and is therefore labelled by symbol.
