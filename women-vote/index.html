Bangladesh 2026 · How men and women voted

How Women Voted: Evidence from Bangladesh’s 2026 Polling-Centre Results

In Bangladesh, men and women often vote at separate centres. Where enough of a seat votes that way, we can count their votes separately. Jamaat-e-Islami did better with women than with men — but only outside the big cities, where the pattern turns the other way.

1 · What this covers

226 seats, not the whole country

Most polling centres are mixed, so a man's vote cannot be told from a woman's. Single-sex centres are only about a quarter of the country's centres — and they are not spread evenly. Some seats have almost none. Reading a gender gap off two or three centres is not reading anything.

So this analysis keeps only the seats where single-sex centres hold at least 10% of the constituency's electorate. That is 226 of 297 seats. 71 seats are dropped entirely.

Read this as a finding about these 226 seats, not about Bangladesh. The seats that survive the cut lean urban, because sex-segregated polling is mostly an urban practice: 77% of city-corporation centres are single-sex against 18% of village (union) centres. 2 · The main finding

The BNP got the same from both. Jamaat did not.

Across the 226 seats, the BNP's share is the same among men and women to within a rounding error. Jamaat's is 1.6 points higher among women.

3 · Is it just religious parties?

No — the other Islamic party did worse with women

If women simply preferred religious parties, Islami Andolan would have gained too. It did not — it lost ground, and so did the others. Jamaat is the only party here that women backed more than men did.

4 · Where the gap actually lives

Villages and small towns, yes. Big cities, the opposite.

The single national number hides a split. In villages and in small towns women gave Jamaat about 2.4 points more than men did. In the big cities they gave it less — and gave the BNP more.

This is the most important line in the whole analysis. It also explains why the national average is as low as it is: roughly a third of the electorate we can see sits in city corporations, where the gap runs backwards and drags the average down.

5 · Could it just be location?

We checked men and women in the same building

Maybe women's centres simply sit in places that like Jamaat. To test that we kept only buildings holding both a men's centre and a women's centre — same address, same street, same neighbourhood — and compared only inside them: 1,204 buildings, 2,813 centres.

Inside the same building, women give both big parties slightly more than men do, because women's ballots go less often to small parties. So the honest measure is what happens to Jamaat's lead over the BNP. Split by kind of place, that measure repeats the pattern above: Jamaat's lead grows among women in villages and towns and shrinks in the cities, all three significant.

6 · The strongest region

The gap is widest in the south-west

Within the seats in scope, the gap is concentrated in the Khulna division — the Jessore–Satkhira–Jhenaidah belt — where women push Jamaat from 46% to past 50%. In a typical seat elsewhere men and women voted almost the same: the median seat gap is only +0.2 points.

7 · The same region won Jamaat its seats

The south-west gave Jamaat much of what it won

That belt is small — 13% of the seats in scope. But Jamaat won 18 of 29 seats there, which is 38% of everything it won inside this scope. Its strongest region and its widest gender gap are the same place.

8 · The key test

It is not only about that one region

A fair question: is this just "Jamaat is strong in Khulna", counted twice? So we split the seats both ways at once. Even outside the belt, the women's gap is about five times bigger in seats Jamaat won than in seats it lost — +2.5 against +0.5 points.

9 · One more thing

Fewer women voted — and they still chose Jamaat more

Turnout at women's centres was about 5 points lower than at men's. So this is not because more women came out. Fewer came, and they still gave Jamaat a bigger share. It is about choice, not turnout.

10 · What if only one side voted?

On women's votes alone, Jamaat leads more of these seats

Count each of the 226 seats using only its men's centres, then only its women's. Jamaat leads 46 on men's votes and 57 on women's. In 15 seats men lean BNP but women lean Jamaat — 4 go the other way.

Read the gap between the two rows, not the absolute numbers. Counted together, the same centres put Jamaat ahead in 54 seats, while the official result gave it 47 of them. The single-sex centres are already friendlier to Jamaat than the full count is, so neither the men's figure nor the women's figure is a forecast of anything.

In short

What to remember

  • Jamaat is the only party here that women backed more than men — by about 1.6 points across the 226 seats. The BNP was the same for both.
  • It is not simply about religious parties. The other Islamic party, Islami Andolan, did worse with women.
  • The gap is a village and small-town pattern. About +2.4 points in both. In the big cities it runs the other way.
  • Location is not the whole story, but it is part of it. Comparing men and women in the same building gives the same split — Jamaat's lead grows among women outside the cities and shrinks inside them.
  • It is concentrated, not national. Almost no gap in the median seat; the widest gaps are in the south-west, where women push Jamaat past 50%.
  • That region also gave Jamaat much of what it won — 18 of 29 seats there, 38% of its wins in scope from 13% of the seats.
  • Fewer women voted, but they still chose Jamaat more — so it is choice, not turnout.
  • This is not a national result. It covers 226 of 297 seats, and inside them only the quarter or so of voters who use single-sex centres — a more urban slice than the country. Those centres lean more to Jamaat than the full count does, so the seat numbers here are not projections.

Scope. Every figure covers the 226 constituencies where single-sex polling centres hold at least 10% of the constituency's electorate. Coverage inside those seats runs from 10% to 98%, median 22%. The other 71 seats — including 7 with no single-sex centre at all — are excluded, because a gender gap measured off a handful of centres is noise. Figures come from 10,468 single-sex centres (5,247 men's, 5,221 women's) holding about 30% of the electorate of the seats in scope. Mixed centres cannot be split by sex and are never used.

Known bias. Sex-segregated polling is mostly an urban practice — 77% of city-corporation centres are single-sex against 18% of village centres — so this sample over-represents cities. Across all 297 seats, counting only single-sex centres understates the BNP by 1.7 points, overstates the NCP by 2.2, and calls a different winner from the full result in 23 of 290 covered seats. That is why seat counts here are presented against the same centres counted together, not against the official result.

Same-building test. Buildings are matched on the exact centre name within a constituency (Bengali letters and digits only). Parenthetical qualifiers such as (বালক শাখা) and (বালিকা শাখা) are kept, since those are separate premises; merging them roughly doubles the sample but mixes different sites and reverses the result, so we do not. Confidence intervals are 95% on the mean of per-building differences. Pooling all types together produces Simpson's paradox — the matched sample is 41% big-city buildings against 31% of the scope's centres — so the test is reported by kind of place, never pooled.

Other definitions. District charts use districts with 6 or more single-sex centres of each sex. "South-west" is the Khulna division: Bagerhat, Chuadanga, Jashore, Jhenaidah, Khulna, Kushtia, Magura, Meherpur, Narail, Satkhira. Seat winners are the full official count, all parties.
