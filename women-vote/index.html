Finding 2 of 7

The women’s edge belongs to villages and small towns

Jamaat’s share was 2.4 pp higher at women-only centres in both. In big cities its gap was not clearly different from zero; BNP’s share was 1.2 pp higher.

Finding 3 of 7

The same-premises check reproduces the divide

At 1,204 matched polling premises, the women–men difference in Jamaat’s lead over BNP favours Jamaat outside big cities—and BNP inside them.

Finding 4 of 7

The pattern remains at coalition level

The Jamaat-led bloc’s share was 2.1 pp higher at women-only village centres and 2.3 higher in towns, but 0.8 lower in big cities.

Finding 5 of 7

The south-west is the hotspot

Across the 29 covered Khulna-division seats, Jamaat had 45.7% at men-only centres and 50.7% at women-only centres. The gap was 1.0 pp across covered seats elsewhere.

Finding 6 of 7

The gap is larger where Jamaat’s 2026 vote share is higher

It is near zero where Jamaat polled below 10%, and +4.5 pp where Jamaat polled above 50% (seat-level r = 0.40).

Finding 7 of 7

Jamaat leads 11 more seats at women-only centres

Jamaat leads 44 seats using men-only centres and 55 using women-only centres. The same centres counted together give 52.

Methodology

Questions & answers

Open a question for the detail behind the charts.

What does this analysis cover—and is it national?

It uses 10,468 men-only or women-only centres in the 226 of 297 constituencies where those centres hold at least 10% of the electorate. They cover 30.4% of voters in those seats. Mixed centres cannot be split by sex.

No: the sample is not nationally representative and none of the seat figures is a projection.

What is the “women’s gap”?

It is a party’s share of valid votes at women-only centres minus its share at men-only centres, in percentage points. These are aggregate centre results; they do not identify individual voters or show anyone switching parties.

Why are villages, towns and big cities shown separately?

They are the official union, municipality and city-corporation classifications. The result changes direction by place, and the observable sample is urban-skewed. Across all 297 constituencies, 18% of village centres are single-sex, compared with 42% in towns and 77% in big cities. Pooling them would hide the central finding.

What is the same-premises check?

Men-only and women-only centre records are matched when they share the same normalized Bengali premises name within a constituency. Parenthetical qualifiers are retained because they can identify separate premises. The test covers 1,204 matched premises containing 2,813 centres.

Its metric is the change in Jamaat’s lead over BNP, not Jamaat’s share alone. The three settlement types are not pooled in the chart or reported comparison because their mix is very different.

How is uncertainty calculated?

The headline, place and coalition ranges come from 3,000 bootstrap replications that resample whole constituencies, preserving within-seat clustering. The same-premises chart reports 95% intervals around mean premise-level differences. When a range crosses zero, the chart uses a grey marker and does not claim a difference.

Does the 10% coverage rule change the result?

The combined number falls as the rule tightens because rural seats drop out first and the sample becomes more urban. Within each type of place, the pattern is stable.

How are coalitions, seat leads and sources defined?

The Jamaat-led bloc comprises Jamaat, NCP, and the rickshaw and wall-clock symbols; the BNP-led bloc comprises BNP and the hat symbol. A seat lead is the largest vote total among the eight symbols recorded in the source—not an official result.

Shares use all valid votes as the denominator. The source files are male_female_centers.csv and election_results_with_geo.csv, derived from published Election Commission centre-level results. Boundary geometry comes from the project's constituency-delimitation file. Presentation outputs are in deck.json, seats_in_scope.csv and seats_map.geojson.
